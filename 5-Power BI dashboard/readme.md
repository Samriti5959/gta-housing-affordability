## Key Findings by KPI

Rent-to-income ratio (highest/lowest): Toronto has the highest ratio in the GTA at 24.6% — meaning even though its raw rent isn't the highest, it's the worst relative to income. Halton Hills has the lowest at 12.1%. But affordability here can be misleading: even in Halton Hills, actual renter stress sits at 40.3%, meaning the "most affordable" municipality by this measure still leaves a large share of renters cost-burdened.

Income needed for a 2-bedroom: Newmarket requires the highest income of any municipality — about $95K — to keep a 2-bedroom under the 30% affordability line, driven by its rent ($2,384) now exceeding Toronto's ($2,055).

Renter stress (30%+ of income on shelter): Richmond Hill has the highest rate at 51.2%, followed closely by Vaughan (50.4%) and Markham (47.8%). These are all higher-income suburbs — the strain is concentrated where the aggregate income data looks strongest, which is exactly what makes it invisible in median-income-only views.

Vacancy rate: Brampton has the most rental choice in the region at 4.6% vacancy, giving renters there more bargaining power than in tighter markets like Toronto.

Rent-vs-income growth gap: Newmarket also shows the widest gap between rent growth and income growth (17.3 percentage points, 2015–2020), reinforcing that its affordability problem isn't just a snapshot — it's a widening trend.

Overall conclusion: GTA housing affordability isn't one number. At median household income, every municipality looks affordable — rent stays under 30% everywhere. But renter-specific data tells a different story: 32–51% of renter households are already cost-burdened depending on the municipality, and the worst strain is often hidden inside the highest-income suburbs, not the city centre. The common assumption that moving further out (Brampton, Halton, Newmarket) automatically means more affordable housing doesn't hold up once income and growth trends are factored in alongside rent.
